African Safari Cost in 2026: Real Prices Per Day and Per Night

What a safari actually costs once you separate the lodge, the park fee and the vehicle, using live rates from our own booking engine rather than brochure estimates.

Short answer

A mid-range African safari costs roughly $150 to $450 per person per day, all in. Budget camping trips run $120 to $200 a day, and luxury lodges run $600 to $1,500 a day or more. Accommodation is the piece you control: on EA Booking, lodges near the Serengeti start around $53 a night and camps around the Masai Mara start around $74 a night, while the famous names in the same reserves run past $2,000.

The rest of the bill is largely fixed. Park entry fees, a 4x4 with a guide and internal transfers cost about the same whether you sleep in a tent or a suite.

What you are actually paying for

Safari pricing confuses people because operators quote one number that hides four very different costs. Split them apart and the trip becomes much easier to control.

That first row is the whole argument for booking your own stays. A group that switches from an inside-the-reserve lodge to a camp twenty minutes outside the gate can cut the accommodation line by half and still be on the first game drive at dawn.

Park fees are the cost nobody warns you about

Park and conservancy fees are charged per person per 24 hours, on top of everything else, and for a family of four they can quietly become the second largest line in the budget. They are set by government bodies, revised regularly, and differ for residents and non-residents.

  • Kenya: national park fees are published by the Kenya Wildlife Service. The Masai Mara is a national reserve run by Narok County rather than KWS, and the private conservancies bordering it charge their own nightly fee on top.
  • Tanzania: Serengeti and the other national parks are run by TANAPA. The Ngorongoro Conservation Area is separate, run by the NCAA, and descending into the crater carries an extra vehicle fee.
  • Uganda: fees are published by the Uganda Wildlife Authority. Gorilla permits are priced separately and sell out months ahead. See our Uganda national parks guide.

Always confirm the current fee on the authority's own site before you budget. Published figures change without much notice, and a fee quoted on a travel blog from two seasons ago is the most common reason a safari budget comes in short.

How to bring the cost down without ruining the trip

Sleep outside the gate

Camps in the towns and community areas just outside a reserve routinely cost a third of what the same standard costs inside it. You trade about thirty to sixty minutes of driving at each end of the day. On a week-long trip that single decision is often worth more than every other saving combined.

Travel in the long rains or the shoulder months

April and May are the cheapest months in both Kenya and Tanzania, and November is close behind. Game viewing is genuinely good, the parks are quiet and lodge rates drop sharply. Our best time to visit Kenya and best time to visit Tanzania pages break this down month by month against price.

Drive instead of flying between parks

Light aircraft transfers between reserves are fast and expensive. A road transfer costs a fraction and, on the Kenyan and northern Tanzanian circuits, the drives are short enough to be worth it.

Share the vehicle

A private 4x4 with a guide is the single biggest fixed cost after the lodge. Four people in one vehicle pay a quarter each of what a couple pays for the same car.

Add a beach week instead of more safari days

Safari days are the most expensive days of any East Africa trip. Zanzibar costs a fraction as much per day, which is why the combination is so popular. We cost that pairing out on our safari and Zanzibar page.

Where East Africa sits against the rest of the continent

Kenya and Tanzania are the most expensive safari destinations in Africa, because the Mara and the Serengeti carry the highest fees and the strongest demand. Uganda is meaningfully cheaper for everything except gorilla permits, and its parks are quieter. Ethiopia is not a conventional big-game destination at all, and travellers go for the northern historic route instead, at a much lower daily cost.

If your priority is the cheapest possible safari rather than a particular animal, look at Uganda's savannah parks and Kenya's smaller reserves such as Tsavo, which you can reach from the coast. Our Diani Beach page covers that coast-plus-safari combination.

Common questions

How much does a safari cost per day?

Between $120 and $200 per person per day for budget camping, $150 to $450 for a mid-range lodge safari, and $700 or more per day at the luxury end. Those totals include your stay, park fees, a 4x4 with a guide and meals, but not international flights.

What is the cheapest African safari?

A camping or budget-lodge safari in Uganda or in Kenya's smaller parks, booked in the April to May low season, sharing a vehicle and sleeping just outside the reserve. Done that way, a week on safari can cost under $1,000 per person excluding flights.

Why are park fees so high?

They fund conservation, anti-poaching and community payments, and they are the main reason a safari costs more than a normal holiday. They are charged per person for each 24 hours you are inside the park and are set by government authorities, not by hotels or booking sites.

Is a safari cheaper in Kenya or Tanzania?

Kenya is usually slightly cheaper overall, mostly because distances are shorter and road transfers are easier. Tanzania's Serengeti and Ngorongoro carry higher combined fees. For a first trip, the deciding factor is normally where the migration is in the month you travel.

Can I book a safari lodge myself instead of buying a package?

Yes, and it is usually cheaper. You book the stays directly, then arrange a vehicle and guide locally or through the lodge. Park fees are paid at the gate or included by the lodge. You lose the single point of contact a package gives you, and you gain full control of the largest cost in the trip.
