Somaliland Guide: Laas Geel, Hargeisa and Entry Rules

Laas Geel holds some of the oldest and best preserved rock art in Africa, and almost nobody has seen it. Here is what is actually involved in getting there.

Short answer

Laas Geel is a complex of painted rock shelters roughly 50 kilometres north east of Hargeisa, holding Neolithic cattle paintings thought to be between 5,000 and 11,000 years old, in extraordinary condition. Visiting means flying into Hargeisa, obtaining the required entry permission, and arranging a permit and escorted transport locally for the site itself.

This is genuinely off the standard tourist map. Plan it as an expedition rather than a holiday, and check your own government's current travel advice for the region before committing to anything.

Why Laas Geel matters

The site was documented by a French archaeological team in 2002, which is very recent for something of this significance. What makes it remarkable is not only the age but the preservation: the pigments are still vivid, because the granite overhangs have sheltered them from rain and direct sun for thousands of years.

The paintings show long-horned cattle in ceremonial dress, human figures with arms raised, dogs and wild animals. They record a period when this landscape was wet enough to sustain herding on a scale it cannot today, which is part of what makes the site scientifically important as well as visually striking.

There is no crowd. Visitor numbers are a tiny fraction of any comparable site anywhere in the world, and most people who go are alone at the shelters.

Getting to Laas Geel

  • Base yourself in Hargeisa. The site is about 50 kilometres away, roughly an hour on the road towards Berbera.
  • You need a permit. Entry to the site requires a permit obtained in Hargeisa, normally from the relevant ministry, and this is usually arranged by whoever organises your transport.
  • Transport is arranged locally and an escort is generally required. This is standard practice for travel outside Hargeisa and is not optional.
  • Allow a full day. There is no infrastructure at the site beyond a caretaker, so bring water, sun protection and everything else you need.

Requirements for permits and escorts change. Confirm the current position with your transport operator and with your own government's travel advice before you travel, not from a guide published a season ago.

Hargeisa

Hargeisa is the largest city and the seat of government, sitting at around 1,300 metres, which makes it cooler than the coast. It is the entry point for the region and where any trip is organised.

Things to do in Hargeisa

  • The livestock market, one of the largest in the Horn of Africa, and the clearest window onto the economy of the region.
  • The central market and the money changers, where stacks of shillings are traded openly on the street, which is one of the more photographed sights in the city.
  • The MiG monument in the city centre, a fighter aircraft mounted on a plinth, commemorating the bombing of the city in the late 1980s.
  • The Hargeisa Cultural Centre, for exhibitions, archives and a sense of the contemporary cultural scene.

Berbera and the coast

Berbera lies on the Gulf of Aden about two and a half hours north of Hargeisa. It has an Ottoman-era old town, a deep water port that has seen major investment, and empty beaches on the Red Sea coast. It is very hot for much of the year, considerably hotter than Hargeisa, and most visitors treat it as a one or two night addition rather than a base.

Entry and permits

Entry requirements for Somaliland are administered separately from the rest of Somalia and are subject to change. Rather than reproduce rules that may be out of date by the time you read this, confirm the current position directly:

  • Entry permission is typically arranged in advance or on arrival at Hargeisa's Egal International Airport, depending on nationality and current policy.
  • Check your own government's travel advice for the region. Several governments advise against travel to parts of Somalia, and the guidance distinguishes between areas, so read which areas it actually covers.
  • Movement outside Hargeisa generally requires a permit and an armed escort, arranged locally.
  • Insurance matters here. Many standard travel policies exclude destinations under a government advisory. Check the wording before you rely on it.

When to go

The cooler, drier months from roughly November to February are the most comfortable. Hargeisa is manageable year round because of the altitude. Berbera and the coast are extremely hot from around May to September and are best avoided then.

Practical notes

  • Cash. Card acceptance is limited. Mobile money is widely used locally, and US dollars are useful.
  • Dress conservatively. This is a conservative Muslim society. Women should expect to cover hair, arms and legs.
  • Alcohol is prohibited.
  • Connectivity is better than expected. Mobile data is widely available and inexpensive in the main towns.
  • Photograph with permission. Ask before photographing people, and avoid government buildings and checkpoints entirely.

Common questions

What is Laas Geel?

A complex of painted rock shelters about 50 kilometres from Hargeisa, holding Neolithic paintings of long-horned cattle, human figures and animals, estimated at between 5,000 and 11,000 years old. The pigments are exceptionally well preserved because the granite overhangs have sheltered them from rain and direct sun.

Do you need a permit to visit Laas Geel?

Yes. A permit is obtained in Hargeisa and travel outside the city generally requires an escort as well. Both are normally arranged by the operator providing your transport. Requirements change, so confirm the current position before you travel.

How do you get to Hargeisa?

By air to Egal International Airport, with regional connections from hubs including Addis Ababa, Dubai and Nairobi. Entry arrangements for the region are administered separately from the rest of Somalia and depend on your nationality and current policy.
